# Problem and Evidence Evaluation

## Purpose

Evaluate whether a product problem is sufficiently supported by evidence to justify solution evaluation.

The goal is not absolute certainty. Determine whether the available evidence supports the current problem hypothesis strongly enough for the next decision.

## 1. Separate the Problem from the Solution

A **problem** is an observed user, business, or organisational condition that creates an undesirable outcome.

> Users struggle to complete campaign configuration.

A **solution disguised as a problem** presents a proposed intervention as though it were the problem.

> Users need an AI assistant to complete campaigns faster.

When the statement is solution-led:

1. Identify the outcome the proposed solution is intended to improve.
2. Frame plausible underlying problem hypotheses.
3. Evaluate each hypothesis against the evidence provided.
4. Do not move to solution evaluation until at least one hypothesis has sufficient support.

Do not assume that a proposed solution is necessary because it may address a plausible problem.

## 2. Frame the Problem Hypothesis

Each hypothesis should specify:

- who experiences the problem
- what happens
- when or under what conditions it occurs
- the consequence or impact
- why it matters

Prefer:

> New users abandon campaign setup during configuration, reducing activation and increasing support requests.

Avoid vague statements such as:

> Users dislike onboarding.

## 3. Use Claim → Evidence → Inference

For every important hypothesis, separate:

```text
Claim      What are we saying is true?
Evidence   What observations support the claim?
Inference  What does the evidence justify concluding?
```

Evaluate the gap between evidence and inference. Do not treat an inference as fact merely because evidence exists.

Example:

```text
Claim:
Users abandon onboarding because configuration is confusing.

Evidence:
8 of 10 interviewed users struggled during configuration.

Inference:
Configuration difficulty may contribute to onboarding abandonment.
The evidence does not independently establish causality.
```

## 4. Evaluate the Evidence

Assess evidence against these dimensions:

### Relevance

Does it directly support the problem hypothesis, or only a related claim?

### Reliability

How trustworthy are the source and collection method? Consider analytics, controlled experiments, repeated research patterns, isolated anecdotes, opinions, and assumptions accordingly.

### Coverage

How well does it represent the affected user, customer, or business group? Distinguish isolated feedback from evidence showing scale.

### Consistency

Do independent sources point to the same problem?

### Causality

Does the evidence establish causation, or only correlation or temporal association? Never infer causality from correlation alone.

## 5. Classify Evidence Types

Use one or more of:

- **Quantitative:** conversion, retention, engagement, revenue, error, or usage metrics
- **Qualitative:** interviews, observations, support conversations, or open-ended feedback
- **Behavioural:** observed product actions and usage patterns
- **Experimental:** controlled tests or other intervention-based evidence
- **Stakeholder input:** informed internal perspective
- **Customer feedback:** reported needs, pain, or outcomes
- **Assumption:** an unverified belief

Stakeholder input and assumptions can generate hypotheses, but are not equivalent to observed evidence.

## 6. Create a Problem Evaluation Record

For each hypothesis, produce:

```text
Problem hypothesis:
Problem evaluation:
Evidence:
Evidence evaluation:
Evidence type:
Confidence:
```

The problem evaluation should state whether the hypothesis is supported, partially supported, unsupported, or contradicted. The evidence evaluation should explain relevance, reliability, coverage, consistency, causality, and important gaps.

## 7. Assess Confidence

Confidence reflects evidence quality, relevance, coverage, consistency, remaining uncertainty, and the strength of the inference.

Use **High**, **Medium**, or **Low**. Do not use numerical percentages without a defensible basis.

- **High:** relevant and sufficiently reliable evidence supports the problem; no critical uncertainty is likely to change its definition.
- **Medium:** meaningful support exists, but important uncertainties remain.
- **Low:** the hypothesis is mainly assumption-led, weakly evidenced, or over-inferred.

## 8. Recommend the Next Action

End with one recommended action and the reason for it.

- **High confidence:** proceed to solution evaluation, unless a critical unresolved uncertainty could materially change the problem.
- **Medium confidence:** collect targeted evidence addressing the most decision-relevant uncertainty.
- **Low confidence:** reframe the problem, investigate alternative hypotheses, or stop/reconsider the initiative.

Possible actions include qualitative research, behavioural or quantitative analysis, prototyping, experimentation, or solution evaluation.

State:

1. why the action is appropriate;
2. which uncertainty it addresses; and
3. what information could change the recommendation.

## 9. Human Review

Present the assessment as advisory. Clearly distinguish:

- what is known;
- what is inferred;
- what remains uncertain;
- what should be validated by relevant stakeholders or domain experts.
